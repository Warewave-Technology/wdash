Local accounts are not a directory: they are checked here, against a password stored as an Argon2 hash, and they keep working when LDAP or OpenID Connect does not. That is what they are for, and it is why the installation refuses to be left without one that is enabled and can administer. python -m wdash.store.recover --status says who that is from the command line, without signing in.
Every local account needs an authenticator as well as a password, set up at its first sign-in and never optional. LDAP and OpenID Connect accounts are not listed here and get no second factor from WDash: it never sees their passwords, and a second factor for them belongs at the provider that checks them. If somebody loses their phone, reset it here, or run python -m wdash.store.recover --reset-totp <username> when nobody can open this page.

These take effect immediately — no restart. Nobody is signed out by a change here, and the local account created at setup keeps working regardless of what happens to the provider. That is what makes this page safe to experiment on: a wrong setting costs the single sign-on button, not your way back in.
A provider switched off is off, even with its settings still filled in. WDash does not fall back to the environment in that case — otherwise the switch would do nothing on a deployment that has both. That is also how an OIDC provider configured in the environment is turned off from here: save the card with Enabled unchecked.
At most one directory signs people in. Either LDAP or OpenID Connect, never both: a dashboard belongs to a username and a role mapping is written against one, so with two directories open a name at one of them belongs to somebody at the other. Enabling the second is refused here; to switch, turn the first one off and save, then enable the other — the second save says what those names already own. python -m wdash.store.recover --use-directory <ldap|oidc|none> does the same from the command line. Local accounts are not a directory and none of this touches them.
